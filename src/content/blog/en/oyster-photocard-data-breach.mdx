**In short:** Transport for London (TfL) says about 265,000 Oyster photocard customers may have had personal details exposed, including the security question and answer on their online account. According to TfL, passwords and payment card details were not affected. If you have a photocard account, change your password and your security answer, change that answer anywhere else you use it, and be wary of messages about TfL or your travel card in the weeks ahead.

## What happened

On Monday 16 November 2026, TfL noticed suspicious activity: customers with photocard accounts were sent emails asking them to create an account. The emails came from TfL's own system, but they were not authorised ([ianVisits](https://www.ianvisits.co.uk/articles/tfl-cyber-attack-may-have-exposed-data-of-265000-oyster-photocard-users-94247/)). TfL says it believes that "around 265,000 photocard account holders may have had their details" compromised ([TfL](https://tfl.gov.uk/contact-us/we-re-doing-maintenance-on-our-photocard-website)).

According to the notices TfL sent to customers, the details exposed may include ([BleepingComputer](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/transport-for-london-breach-exposes-265-000-oyster-card-users-data/)):

- full name;
- email address and phone number;
- home address;
- date of birth;
- the account's security question and answer.

TfL's current assessment is that "no passwords, bank or payment card details, customer journey information, names and dates of birth of any children with a Zip Oyster card, photographs and copies of any supporting documentation have been compromised" ([TfL](https://tfl.gov.uk/contact-us/we-re-doing-maintenance-on-our-photocard-website)). It has found no evidence so far that the data has been misused. TfL has told the Information Commissioner's Office, taken the photocard website offline, and says photocards can still be used to travel. The investigation is not finished, so the number of people affected may change.

The cards involved include Zip Oyster, 18+ Student, 18-25 Care Leaver, 60+ London, Apprentice and Veterans Oyster photocards.

## Why the security answer matters most

A password can be changed in a minute. A security answer is often a fact about your life, such as a first school or a mother's maiden name, and the same answer is often used on many sites. BleepingComputer notes that the leak of security questions and answers "is particularly concerning, as those may be used in account access restoration". Someone who knows your answer may try to use it to reset the password on another account.

This weakness is well known. The US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) now tells services that they "SHALL NOT prompt subscribers to use knowledge-based authentication (KBA)" or security questions when people choose passwords ([NIST SP 800-63B](https://pages.nist.gov/800-63-4/sp800-63b/authenticators/)). Many older sites still use them, so it is worth knowing where yours are.

## What to do now if you have an Oyster photocard account

1. **Ignore the earlier "create an account" email.** BleepingComputer reports that people who received it "should ignore it". Do not use links in any message about the breach: go to the TfL website yourself.
2. **Change your password and your security answer.** The photocard website was still offline on 23 November 2026. TfL says it will email affected customers once the website is restored, and then ask them "to change your password, security question and answer". Do it as soon as the website is back.
3. **Change the same answer, and the same password, everywhere else.** If you used them on any other site, change them there too. The UK National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) advises that "if any of your other accounts use the same password, you should change them as well" ([NCSC: Data breaches](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/guidance/data-breaches)).
4. **Turn on 2-step verification where you can.** The NCSC says it "helps to keep cyber criminals out of your accounts, even if they know your passwords" ([NCSC: Turn on 2-step verification](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/collection/top-tips-for-staying-secure-online/activate-2-step-verification-on-your-email)). It matters most for your email account, because that is where password resets are sent.
5. **Keep an eye on your accounts.** The NCSC suggests looking for "logins or attempted logins from strange locations or at unusual times". If an account has been taken over, its guide to [recovering a hacked account](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/guidance/recovering-a-hacked-account) goes step by step.

For renewals or journey history while the website is offline, TfL Customer Services can help on 0343 222 1234.

## Expect scams that use your details

A leak like this does not end with the leak. A name, a home address and a date of birth make a scam message far more convincing. The NCSC warns that scam messages can follow a breach, sometimes long after it, and reminds people that "your bank (or any other official organisation) will never ask you to supply personal information" ([NCSC: Data breaches](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/guidance/data-breaches)).

- **Watch for pressure.** One warning sign the NCSC lists is "being urged to act immediately or within a limited timeframe".
- **Contact organisations yourself.** Use the website address or phone number you already know, not the links or numbers in the message.
- **Report what you receive.** Forward scam texts to 7726. Forward scam emails to the NCSC; the address is on its [Report a scam email](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/collection/phishing-scams/report-scam-email) page.
- **If you lose money,** the NCSC advises you to "tell your bank and report it as a crime to Report Fraud".

[Does encryption protect against hackers?](https://doughnut-forge.app/blog/does-encryption-protect-against-hackers/) explains why phishing gets around even strong encryption, and what helps instead.

## Every account is a copy of you

No one can undo this breach from their own phone or computer, and no app on your device would have changed it: the details were held by TfL. That is true of most of the places your details live, from a bank to an online shop. [Breaches keep happening. Here is what you can still control](https://doughnut-forge.app/blog/protect-personal-files-from-data-breaches/) looks at that side of the problem.

What you can choose is how many copies of yourself you hand out. Each account you open usually asks for a name, an email address, perhaps a home address, a date of birth or a security answer, and each one is kept by someone else. Before signing up, it is worth asking two questions:

- **Does this service need an account at all?** Some tools work without one.
- **Does it need every detail on the form?** If a field is optional, you can leave it blank.

[Doughnut Forge](https://doughnut-forge.app/) is one example of a tool that needs no account. It encrypts files and text on your own device, and only you and the people you choose can open them. There are no Doughnut Forge servers and no account to create, so none of your files or details are stored anywhere else. The app collects nothing. It would not have protected anyone's TfL details, and it does not stop phishing: it is for the private files you keep or share yourself.

## Questions people ask

**Were Oyster photocard passwords stolen?** TfL says no passwords were compromised. It will still ask affected customers to change their password and choose a new security question and answer.

**Were bank or payment card details exposed?** TfL says no bank or payment card details were compromised.

**Can I still use my photocard?** Yes. TfL says customers "can continue to travel".

**Is my child's Zip Oyster information affected?** TfL says the names and dates of birth of children with Zip Oyster cards were not compromised. Other details on the account, such as the contact details, may have been.

**What should I do if someone contacts me about the breach?** Do not reply or follow any link. Contact TfL yourself, using contact details you already know, and report the message.
