**In short:** on 4 November 2026, three government security agencies published a set of questions for organisations to ask their suppliers about post-quantum encryption. Behind it sits a risk often called "harvest now, decrypt later": encrypted data copied today could be opened years from now, if quantum computers become powerful enough. For most everyday files this is not a reason to worry. For the files you expect to keep private for many years, it is worth knowing what to ask of the apps you trust with them.

## What was published this week

The US National Security Agency (NSA), the Australian Signals Directorate's Australian Cyber Security Centre (ASD's ACSC) and the US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) released guidance called "Post-quantum questions to ask your vendors: Evaluating third-party readiness for post-quantum cryptography" ([NSA press release, 4 November 2026](https://www.nsa.gov/Press-Room/Press-Releases-Statements/Press-Release-View/Article/4641375/nsa-joins-asds-acsc-and-cisa-to-provide-practical-questions-to-evaluate-third-p/)).

It is written for organisations: for buying, for contract renewals and for checking existing suppliers. Its starting point is simple. Most organisations rely on products made by others, so their move to post-quantum encryption "will depend on its vendors' readiness, transparency, and roadmaps." The release warns that leaving the questions too late risks "discovering a critical product, service, or dependency cannot support PQC within required timeframes."

PQC stands for post-quantum cryptography: encryption designed to resist attack by quantum computers. [What quantum-safe encryption means, in plain language](/blog/quantum-safe-encryption-explained/) explains how it works. This post looks at the threat behind the guidance, and at the timing.

## What "harvest now, decrypt later" means

Encrypted data can be copied and kept, even by someone who cannot read it today. The UK National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) describes the risk of "an attacker collecting and storing data today and decrypting it at some point in the future" ([NCSC: Next steps in preparing for post-quantum cryptography](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/whitepaper/next-steps-preparing-for-post-quantum-cryptography)).

It is a bet on the future. The copy cannot be opened now. But the NCSC warns that, with a large enough quantum computer, "most traditional public key cryptography (PKC) algorithms in use today will be vulnerable to attack." Those are the methods most apps use to pass the key to a file safely to the right person. If that step can be broken later, a copy taken today could be opened later.

## Two clocks

Whether this matters for a given file depends on two things.

- **How long the file has to stay private.** A shopping list stops mattering within a week. A will, a medical history, scans of identity documents or legal papers may need to stay private for decades.
- **When a capable quantum computer might exist.** No one knows. The NCSC says it is "hard to predict when this may happen as many engineering and physical challenges must be overcome first."

If a file has to stay private for longer than it takes such a computer to arrive, a copy taken today becomes a problem later. Because no one can read the second clock, the careful choice for long-lived files is to protect them as if it might run out first.

## How worried should you be?

Not very, for most things. The NCSC notes that such an attack "is only likely to be worthwhile for very high-value information." Keeping vast amounts of encrypted data for years costs money, and someone has to want a particular set of data enough to keep it.

The same NCSC paper points to stronger protection for "particularly sensitive data that needs to be kept secure for a long period of time." So this is not a reason to panic about every file. It is a reason to choose carefully how your most private, longest-lived files are locked.

## The timetable for organisations

Governments are already planning the move. On 13 August 2024, the US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) published its first finalised post-quantum standards, and encouraged system administrators "to begin transitioning to the new standards as soon as possible" ([NIST](https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2024/08/nist-releases-first-3-finalized-post-quantum-encryption-standards)).

The NCSC sets out milestones in [Timelines for migration to post-quantum cryptography](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/guidance/pqc-migration-timelines):

- **By 2028:** "Build an initial plan for migration".
- **By 2031:** "Carry out your early, highest-priority PQC migration activities".
- **By 2035:** "Complete migration to PQC of all your systems, services and products".

These dates are aimed mainly at large organisations and the operators of critical systems, not at individuals. They do show two things: the risk is taken seriously, and moving everything across will take years.

## Questions to ask of any app you trust with long-lived files

The agencies' questions are written for businesses buying products. The same idea works for anyone choosing an app for private files. These are not the agencies' own questions, but they follow the same thinking:

1. **Does it say plainly whether it uses post-quantum encryption?** A clear statement in its own description is a good sign. Words like "unbreakable" are not: no honest app can promise that.
2. **What does the post-quantum part protect?** The part that matters most is how the key to your file is protected, because that is the step quantum computers threaten.
3. **Does it use a hybrid?** Many apps pair a long-trusted method with a newer post-quantum one, so both would have to be broken to get at the key.
4. **Who holds the keys?** If a service keeps the keys for you, its protection, not only the encryption, decides who can open your files.
5. **Has it had an independent security audit, and does it say so plainly?** An honest "not yet" tells you more than a vague claim.
6. **Does it say what it does not protect against?** Every app has limits. A trustworthy one lists them.

## What post-quantum encryption cannot do

- **It cannot reach copies made in the past.** A file sent or stored earlier with older encryption, or with none, stays as it was. Locking a new copy does not change the old ones.
- **It does not protect a file once it is open.** Anyone you choose to share a file with can still copy what is inside.
- **It cannot rescue lost keys.** If you lose your password and your 24-word backup phrase, your files cannot be opened, now or in the future.

## The short version

"Harvest now, decrypt later" is a real risk, but a narrow one: it matters most for valuable information that has to stay private for a long time. This week's guidance shows organisations being told to ask their suppliers hard questions about it. The same questions are worth asking of any app you trust with the files you plan to keep for years.
