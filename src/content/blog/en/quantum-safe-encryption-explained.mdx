**In short:** a large enough quantum computer could break most of the methods used today to pass encryption keys safely between people. Today's quantum computers are still limited. Quantum-safe encryption, also called post-quantum encryption, uses methods designed to resist one. In 2024, the US standards body NIST published the first official standards for it.

## Two kinds of lock

Everyday encryption usually uses two kinds of lock together.

- **The lock on the data.** The file itself is scrambled with a secret key. This kind is fast and very strong.
- **The lock on the key.** The secret key then has to reach the right person without anyone else seeing it. This is done with public-key encryption: anyone can lock something for you with your public key, but only your private key opens it.

Think of a locked box (your file) and the key to that box. The box is solid. The weak point, if there is one, is the envelope the key travels in.

## What a quantum computer could break, and what it could not

A quantum computer works in a very different way from the computers in use today. In theory, it can do certain calculations that ordinary computers cannot do in any practical time, and some of those are exactly what today's public-key encryption relies on being hard. When such a machine will exist is, in the words of the UK National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC), "hard to predict", because "many engineering and physical challenges must be overcome first."

The NCSC puts it plainly: "most traditional public key cryptography (PKC) algorithms in use today will be vulnerable to attack" by a large enough quantum computer. The lock on the data is a different story. The NCSC says "the security of symmetric cryptography is not significantly impacted by quantum computers", and that existing methods such as AES with keys of at least 128 bits can continue to be used ([NCSC: Next steps in preparing for post-quantum cryptography](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/whitepaper/next-steps-preparing-for-post-quantum-cryptography)).

So the box stays solid. It is the envelope that needs replacing.

## Why it matters before such computers exist

If no quantum computer can do this yet, why change anything now? Because encrypted data can be copied and kept. The NCSC describes the risk of "an attacker collecting and storing data today and decrypting it at some point in the future".

The NCSC also notes that such an attack is "only likely to be worthwhile for very high-value information". For most people, this is not a reason to worry. It is a reason to prefer encryption that will last, especially for files you expect to keep for years: legal papers, medical records, private letters.

## What "quantum-safe" and "post-quantum" mean

They mean the same thing: encryption methods designed to resist attack by quantum computers. "Post-quantum" is the term standards bodies use; "quantum-safe" is a common everyday name for it.

On 13 August 2024, the US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) published its first three finalised post-quantum standards: one for general encryption and two for digital signatures ([NIST: first 3 finalized post-quantum encryption standards](https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2024/08/nist-releases-first-3-finalized-post-quantum-encryption-standards)). NIST encourages organisations to start moving to them now, "because full integration will take time".

## Why many apps use a hybrid

Post-quantum methods are newer than the ones they replace. A common approach is a hybrid: protect the key with a long-trusted method **and** a post-quantum one together. Someone would have to break both to get at the key.

## What this means for you

- **There is no need to panic.** No one can say when a quantum computer able to do this will exist, and the lock on your data itself is not the weak point.
- **For files you keep for a long time,** post-quantum protection is worth having now rather than later.
- **Look for plain claims.** An app should say clearly whether it uses post-quantum encryption. Be wary of words like "unbreakable": no honest app can promise that.
