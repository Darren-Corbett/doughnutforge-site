**Usually not all of them.** Most apps from the official app store are not worth panicking about, but some are worth a closer look. Two kinds are worth deleting: apps installed from outside the official store, and apps you no longer use. For the rest, the best fix is usually to change what the app is allowed to see, not to delete it. Both your iPhone and your Android phone can show you what an app collects and what it has been using.

## How to check what an app collects

Start with what the app says about itself, then check what it actually does on your phone.

**On iPhone and iPad**

- **Before you install:** open the app's page in the App Store and scroll to **App Privacy**. The label sorts data into groups such as "Data Linked to You" and "Data Not Linked to You". Tap **See Details** for the full list.
- **After you install:** turn on **App Privacy Report**. Go to **Settings > Privacy & Security > App Privacy Report** and tap **Turn on App Privacy Report**. It needs iOS or iPadOS 15.2 or later. It shows how often apps used your location, camera, microphone and other data in the past seven days, and which web domains they contacted. Apple says the report is encrypted and stored only on your device. It starts empty, so look again after a few days.

**On Android**

- **Before you install:** open the app in Google Play and find **Data safety**. Tap it for the details: which types of data the app collects, why, and whether it shares them.
- **After you install:** open the **Privacy dashboard**. Go to **Settings**, tap **Security and Privacy** or **Privacy**, then **Privacy dashboard**. Pick a permission, such as location or the microphone, to see which apps used it. It covers the last seven days on Android 13 and later, and the last 24 hours on Android 12. Menu names can differ a little from one phone maker to another.

## A label is a starting point, not proof

Apple's and Google's labels are written by the app's developer, not by Apple or Google. Apple tells developers: "You're responsible for keeping your responses accurate and up to date." Apple's own help page says the information "is self-reported by the developer". Google says its Data safety section "is based on information declared by app developers", and that it can differ from the permissions the app asks for.

The rules also leave room. On Google Play, data that an app uses only on your phone, and never sends off it, does not have to be listed as collected. That is fair, because it never leaves your hands, but it means a short label is not the whole story.

The same is true of the app this site is about: [Doughnut Forge](https://doughnut-forge.app/), an app for encrypting files on your own device, shows "Data Not Collected" on the App Store, and that too is the developer's own statement. That is why the report on your phone matters. It shows what an app actually did, not just what its maker says.

## When deleting is worth it

**Apps installed from outside the official store.** The UK National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) advises: "Only download apps from official stores or websites." Its reason: apps in your phone's built-in store "are vetted before they're released". Apps installed from elsewhere skip that check. A recent example shows why this matters. On 10 November 2026, the crypto exchange Binance warned iPhone users about a flaw in some versions of iOS that could let a malicious app read other apps' files. That app has to be installed on the phone first, and an attacker could hand it out outside the App Store. Binance advised deleting unneeded and untrusted apps and updating iOS and every app. No losses had been confirmed when BleepingComputer reported it. If you cannot remember where an app came from, or a website or a message talked you into installing it, delete it.

**Apps you no longer use.** The NCSC is plain about this too: "If there are apps or software on your devices that you no longer use, you should delete them." An unused app can still hold permissions, and it is one more thing to keep updated. If you need it again, it will still be in the store.

**Before you delete, think about the account.** Google points out that deleting an app stops *future* data collection. It does not close your account or remove what the company already holds. If you want that gone, look for a way to delete your account in the app's settings or on the company's website first.

## When changing permissions is enough

For an app you use and like, the permissions are usually the real question. A maps app needs your location to give directions. A photo editor does not need your contacts.

- **iPhone:** go to **Settings > Privacy & Security** and pick a type of data, such as **Location Services**, **Contacts** or **Photos**, to see which apps can use it and turn them off one by one. To stop apps asking to track you across other companies' apps, go to **Settings > Privacy & Security > Tracking** and turn off **Allow Apps to Request to Track**.
- **Android:** go to **Settings > Apps**, choose the app, then **Permissions**, and set each one to **Allow** or **Don't allow**. Under **Unused app settings**, turn on **Pause app activity if unused**, so the phone takes back permissions from apps you stop opening.

If the report on your phone shows an app using your data "in a way or at a time you didn't expect", in Apple's words, change its permission. If it keeps happening, that is a good reason to delete it.

## Why viral "delete these apps" lists often mislead

A short video or post that names apps to delete can sound urgent. Before you act on one, check three things.

1. **The date.** A list can be old by the time it reaches you, and the apps on it may have changed since it was made.
2. **The source.** Good lists say where they came from. In September 2026, PCMag reported research by an IT firm that analysed the privacy policies of more than 5,000 App Store apps. Even PCMag pointed out that "companies self-report all of this information to Apple". A list built on labels is only as good as the labels.
3. **What the app needs.** PCMag also notes that delivery, map and weather apps need your location to work. Collecting data is not the same as misusing it. What matters is whether the data fits what the app does for you.

The question to ask is not "Is this app on a list?" but "Does this app take more than it needs, and did it come from a store I trust?" Your own phone can answer both.

## Keep your phone updated

Updates fix the flaws that bad apps and attackers rely on. The NCSC says: "Applying security updates promptly will help protect your devices and accounts from cyber criminals." It advises turning on automatic updates "if available", for the phone and its apps. Phone makers eventually stop sending updates to older models, so the NCSC suggests avoiding phones that are no longer supported where you can.

It also says that you do not need antivirus software on a smartphone or tablet "provided that you only install apps and software from official stores".

## A five-minute check

1. Update your phone and turn on automatic updates.
2. Delete any app you did not get from the official store, and any app you have not opened in months.
3. Turn on App Privacy Report (iPhone) or open the Privacy dashboard (Android).
4. Turn off location, camera, microphone, contacts and photos for apps that do not need them.
5. Before your next download, read the app's privacy label in the store.

None of this needs a list from social media. Your phone already shows what each app collects and what it has been using.
